## Key question 1 — Gurley: the 10 factors, how he explains each, and his examples

### Takeaway
All ten factors are now verified word for word against the essay: (1) New Experience vs. the Status Quo, (2) Economic Advantages vs. the Status Quo, (3) Opportunity for Technology to Add Value, (4) High Fragmentation, (5) Friction of Supplier Sign-Up, (6) Size of the Market Opportunity, (7) Expand the Market, (8) Frequency, (9) Payment Flow, (10) Network Effects. Gurley gives no weights and no formal ranking — he scores by count ("a 7 or 8 out of 10" against "3 or 4 out of 10") — but he singles out demand aggregation as "much harder and more critical" than supply, fragmentation as "a huge positive", network effects as "rare but golden", and says market size cannot rescue a market where the other factors are negative. The words "rake" and "take rate" do not appear in the essay, and it contains no fee percentage.

### Cited Findings
All items in this section are [V]: read in full in the local copy of the essay. Citation for every bullet: [Gurley][g] (Above the Crowd, November 13, 2012). Typos in the original are kept and marked [sic].

**Introduction — what makes a marketplace work**
- [V] Benchmark's vantage point: "Since Benchmark’s investment in Ebay 15 years ago, we have been fascinated by online marketplaces." The opportunity is to use "the connective tissue of the Internet" to "link the players in a particular market, reducing friction in both the buying and selling experience"; the smartphone amplifies it because participants are "connected to the marketplace 24×7". — [Gurley][g], introduction
- [V] Successes named: "OpenTable, Yelp, Zillow, oDesk, GrubHub, 1stdibs, UShip, and Uber have all reached significant scale within their respective markets." Failures are acknowledged but not named: "we have also invested in several companies that we thought had marketplace opportunities that simply did not play out as expected. Simply put, some industries are much more susceptible to the arrival and success of online marketplaces than others." — [Gurley][g], introduction
- [V] The core test, both sides: "A true marketplace needs natural pull on both the consumer and supplier side of the market. Aggregating suppliers is a necessary, but insufficient step on its own. You must also organically aggregate demand. With each step, it should get easier to acquire the incremental consumer AS WELL AS the incremental supplier." — [Gurley][g], introduction
- [V] Tipping: "Highly liquid marketplaces naturally “tip” towards becoming a clearinghouse where neither the consumer nor the supplier would favor an alternative. That only happens if your momentum is increasing". — [Gurley][g], introduction
- [V] He introduces the list as "10 factors to consider when evaluating the potential success of a new marketplace opportunity". — [Gurley][g], introduction

**The ten factors**
- [V] **1. New Experience vs. the Status Quo.** "Great marketplaces do not simply aggregate a market; they enhance it. They leverage the connective tissue to offer the consumer a user experience that simply was not possible before the arrival of this new intermediary." Examples: OpenTable lets the consumer "search reservation availability across hundreds and hundreds of restaurants in a matter of seconds", against "the incumbent experience (dialing restaurants one by one)"; Uber, "By aggregating thousands of licensed limousine drivers, and overlaying that with a new-age supply chain management solution"; GrubHub's “Track Your Grub”, announced that day. Why it matters: "When this experience delta is great enough, it creates “wow” moments for new users. “Wow” moments lead to word-of-mouth viral growth and high net promoter scores." — [Gurley][g], factor 1
- [V] **2. Economic Advantages vs. the Status Quo.** "Some marketplaces provide enhanced economic advantages." oDesk: purchasers "procure a cheaper alternative, while also providing brand-new economic lift to the programmer (supplier). Both sides experience an economic advantage." Airbnb: "For the property owner, the income is “found money” that simply didn’t exist prior to the marketplace. And in many cases the consumer receives a better price as well." Why it matters: "If you can positively change the economics of an industry, you will find the participants on both sides rooting for your success. This gives you a huge head start when it comes to tipping the marketplace." — [Gurley][g], factor 2
- [V] **3. Opportunity for Technology to Add Value.** "Zillow provides homebuyers with an abundance of data that was historically kept in proprietary systems." Uber: "the system has “perfect” information in an industry where just two years ago there was a complete lack of visibility (on both sides of the network) that led to enormous waste of resources. Uber’s system enables higher car utilization, more fares per hour for the driver, and faster and faster pickup times for the consumer." oDesk is the workflow example: "The marketplace is also a work-flow system that enhances the overall experience for all parties. Facilitating work-flow reduces work for the participants, as well as increasing switching costs." — [Gurley][g], factor 3
- [V] **4. High Fragmentation.** Applies to both sides: "High buyer and supplier fragmentation is a huge positive for an online marketplace. Likewise, a concentrated supplier (or purchaser) base greatly diminishes the likelihood of a successful online marketplace. A highly concentrated supplier base will be reluctant to allow a new intermediary in their market, and as a result will likely fight rather than support your arrival. They will also be very reluctant to share in the economics of the industry". Example: "The large airlines have all but obliterated the economics of online ticketing marketplaces, leading all the online players to focus on hotels where the fragmentation and therefore the economics are higher." Benchmark's successes show "a common theme of fragmented supplier base". — [Gurley][g], factor 4
- [V] **5. Friction of Supplier Sign-Up.** "In some markets signing up suppliers is relative [sic] easy. In others, it can be a painfully slow process that requires lots of touch and local presence." Easy: "At companies such as Yelp, Uber, and GrubHub, new city launches are relatively quick after a process model had been established". Hard: OpenTable, "where the installation of a personal computer and internet connectivity were part of the early roll-out requirements." Double edge: "High friction supplier signup can be a barrier to entry (as it is for OpenTable) if you are able to build a successful marketplace. But in the early stages, this friction slows your roll-out and increases the costs associated with supplier aggregation." Priority remark: "Remember, however, that supplier aggregation is the easy part. Aggregating demand is much harder and more critical." — [Gurley][g], factor 5
- [V] **6. Size of the Market Opportunity.** "A proper TAM (total available market) analysis is imperative, but it is easy to make mistakes looking only at TAM. As a starter, if all the other factors are negative, it will not matter that the market is large. Some markets are crappy candidates for marketplaces. Second, you should also consider the percentage of the market that is likely to use the online alternative." Over-estimate example: healthcare is "unquestionably a very large market. However, the oligopoly of large players in this market controls a massive percentage of market and is unlikely to support a new alternative." Under-estimate example: "In the case of OpenTable many investors missed the opportunity by mistakenly assuming the TAM was too low"; "OpenTable recently passed 10 million diners a month with less than 20% of transactions in North America currently online." Rule: "You must combine a TAM analysis with the likelihood of marketplace success and penetration." — [Gurley][g], factor 6
- [V] **7. Expand the Market.** Framed as a further TAM error: failing to see that the marketplace "may actual [sic] expand the market opportunity for the whole industry"; "certain marketplaces do indeed expand the market — by exploring new price points or enhancing convenience or usability." oDesk: "oDesk’s presence increases the number of first time software outsourcers." Uber: "Uber’s ease of use and simplicity have led many of its users to greatly increase the number of times they use an alternative car service. Some customers now use it as a second car alternative"; this expansion "is in turn a huge boon to the suppliers that share in the economic expansion." — [Gurley][g], factor 7
- [V] **8. Frequency.** "All things being equal, a higher frequency is obviously better. Yelp, GrubHub, OpenTable, 1stdibs (for the designer) and Uber are all high frequency use cases, where the consumers can rely on the marketplace as a utility. Many failed marketplaces attack purchasing cycles that are simply way too infrequent, which makes it much more difficult to build brand awareness and word-of-mouth customer growth." Second trap: "attacking verticals where a satisfactory supplier “match” end’s [sic] the customer’s need to re-enter the market in search of an alternative", which hurts "many vertical service provider markets (such as pediatricians)" where customers prefer "a monogamous relationship". No numeric frequency threshold is given. — [Gurley][g], factor 8
- [V] **9. Payment Flow.** "All things being equal, being part of the payment flow is superior to not being a part of the payment flow. This is due to the fact that it is much easier to extract reasonable economics when you are in the flow of payment. The supplier not only looks to you as a provider of revenue, but they receive that revenue “net of the fee.”" The alternative: "a marketplace where you add value first, and then send a bill to the supplier at later date for services rendered. In this latter case the marketplace appears as an expense, and it’s easier for the supplier to view it is [sic] a “tax” versus a distribution relationship. Cash is king, and if you bring the cash, you are king." Limit: "some industries (like autos) just are not set up for this type of arrangement, as the payment likely lives at the end of a long purchasing process." He names no marketplace as inside or outside the payment flow and states no fee level. — [Gurley][g], factor 9
- [V] **10. Network Effects.** "Network effects are tricky and hard to describe but fundamentally turn on the following question: Can the marketplace provide a better experience to customer “n+1000” than it did to customer “n” directly as a function of adding 1000 more participants to the market? You can pose this question to either side of the network – demand or supply. If you have something like this in place it is magic, as you will get stronger over time not weaker." OpenTable: "we noticed that the reservations per restaurant in a given city were correlated to market penetration." Uber: more "cars on the road, and along with their investment in route and load optimization, this allows for shorter and shorter pickup times." Also: "UGC sites like Yelp and TripAdvisor also have strong network effects. Network effects are rare but golden. If you don’t have one try to find one; if you do have one, try to enhance it." — [Gurley][g], factor 10

**Conclusion — scoring and the limits of the list**
- [V] Scoring by count, not weight: "It is unlikely that you will find a marketplace opportunity that would score ten out of ten with respect to this list. However a 7 or 8 out of 10 would imply that your opportunity of success is much, much higher than if you only match 3 or 4 out of 10." — [Gurley][g], conclusion
- [V] The list is necessary, not sufficient: "finding a great opportunity is only a start, and this analysis could easily mislead one into underestimating the critical role that execution plays when it comes to marketplace businesses. Great marketplace execution is more nuanced and less systematic than other venture backed categories, and for every successful marketplace, you will find an amazing entrepreneur that out-executed the many others that had chosen to attack the same market. In addition to great marketplace characteristics, you also need a world-class entrepreneur to make the dream come true." — [Gurley][g], conclusion

**Quantitative remarks — complete list (the essay has few)**
- [V] "hundreds and hundreds of restaurants in a matter of seconds" (factor 1); "thousands of licensed limousine drivers" (factor 1); "just two years ago" for Uber's industry lacking visibility (factor 3); "10 million diners a month with less than 20% of transactions in North America currently online" (factor 6); customer “n+1000” against customer “n” (factor 10); "7 or 8 out of 10" against "3 or 4 out of 10" (conclusion); "15 years ago" for the eBay investment (introduction). There is no take-rate figure, no frequency threshold, no fragmentation measure and no market-size threshold anywhere in the essay. — [Gurley][g]

### Inferences
- The framework is two-sided by construction, and the text says so factor by factor: experience is judged for the consumer (1), economics for both sides (2), fragmentation for buyers and suppliers (4), network effects on "either side of the network" (10).
- Reading the emphatic phrases together gives an implied priority, which is the researcher's reading and not a ranking Gurley states: demand-side pull first ("much harder and more critical"), then fragmentation ("huge positive") and network effects ("rare but golden"); market size is a gate that cannot compensate for the rest.
- Gurley's own scoring method is a simple count of factors matched. A weighted score would be an addition by the analyst.
- For a non-marketplace business the factors that transfer most directly are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 and 8; factors 5, 9 and 10 are specific to intermediaries. This is the researcher's judgement; the essay speaks only of marketplaces.
- Factor 4 doubles as a risk test for an entrant in a small, concentrated economy: where a few large suppliers or buyers control the market, the text predicts they "will likely fight rather than support your arrival". Applying that to Israel is the analyst's extension; the essay does not mention Israel.

- The essay points to a companion post applying the list to DogVacay ("Our Most Recent Marketplace Investment, DogVacay"); it was not available.
- The essay names no failed marketplace investments, so there is no negative case study to learn from in this source.

## Key question 2 — Graham: principles relevant to an owner-operator, and his quantitative criteria

### Takeaway
Every Graham item below was found in the text and is graded [V]; all the numbers recalled in the first session were confirmed (current ratio 2:1, ten years of earnings, twenty of dividends, one-third growth, 15 × earnings, 1.5 × book, product 22.5). Graham writes for buyers of listed securities, not for founders: the book contains no guidance on starting a business. Its bridge to an owner-operator is his own framing — "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike" — and four "business principles" phrased as rules for running a business. The entrepreneur questions added under each item are the researcher's adaptation, not Graham's words.

### Cited Findings
All items are [V]. Source for every bullet: [Graham][ii] — *The Intelligent Investor*, revised edition (Fourth Revised Edition text of 1973 with Zweig's 2003 commentary; see key question 5). "ch." = Graham's chapter; "p." = book page where a running page number was visible in the text; "L" = line in the local text file. Lines starting "Adaptation (not Graham)" are the researcher's translation into a question for an entrepreneur entering an industry in Israel.

**(a) Investment versus speculation**
- [V] The definition, which Graham quotes from his 1934 textbook *Security Analysis*: "An investment operation is one which, upon thorough analysis promises safety of principal and an adequate return. Operations not meeting these requirements are speculative." He adds: "we have clung tenaciously to this definition over the ensuing 38 years". — [Graham][ii], ch. 1, p. 18 (L1043–1048)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Has my analysis of this venture been thorough enough to show both that the capital I put in is reasonably protected and that the return is adequate — and if not, am I willing to call it a speculation and size it accordingly?
- [V] Margin of safety as the dividing line: "We suggest that the margin-of-safety concept may be used to advantage as the touchstone to distinguish an investment operation from a speculative one." Speculators' claims to a margin "rest on subjective judgment, unsupported by any body of favorable evidence or any conclusive line of reasoning." Conclusion: "to have a true investment there must be present a true margin of safety. And a true margin of safety is one that can be demonstrated by figures, by persuasive reasoning, and by reference to a body of actual experience." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, pp. 519–520 (L22279–22330)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Can I show my safety cushion in three ways — in numbers, in an argument that would persuade a sceptic, and in the actual record of comparable businesses in Israel — or is it only my own feeling that the timing and my skill are good?

**(b) The four business principles (final chapter, section "To Sum Up")**
- [V] Framing: "Investment is most intelligent when it is most businesslike. It is amazing to see how many capable businessmen try to operate in Wall Street with complete disregard of all the sound principles through which they have gained success in their own undertakings. Yet every corporate security may best be viewed, in the first instance, as an ownership interest in, or a claim against, a specific business enterprise. And if a person sets out to make profits from security purchases and sales, he is embarking on a business venture of his own, which must be run in accordance with accepted business principles if it is to have a chance of success." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 523 (L22449–22458)
- [V] Principle 1: "The first and most obvious of these principles is, “Know what you are doing—know your business.” For the investor this means: Do not try to make “business profits” out of securities—that is, returns in excess of normal interest and dividend income—unless you know as much about security values as you would need to know about the value of merchandise that you proposed to manufacture or deal in." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 523 (L22459–22465)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Do I know this industry's unit economics, customers and suppliers as well as someone who already makes or sells the product in Israel — and if not, what would it take to get there before committing capital?
- [V] Principle 2: "A second business principle: “Do not let anyone else run your business, unless (1) you can supervise his performance with adequate care and comprehension or (2) you have unusually strong reasons for placing implicit confidence in his integrity and ability.” For the investor this rule should determine the conditions under which he will permit someone else to decide what is done with his money." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 523 (L22466–22471)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): If a partner, hired manager, franchisor or key supplier will effectively run part of this business, can I competently supervise them, or do I have unusually strong grounds to trust both their integrity and their ability?
- [V] Principle 3: "A third business principle: “Do not enter upon an operation—that is, manufacturing or trading in an item—unless a reliable calculation shows that it has a fair chance to yield a reasonable profit. In particular, keep away from ventures in which you have little to gain and much to lose.” For the enterprising investor this means that his operations for profit should be based not on optimism but on arithmetic. For every investor it means that when he limits his return to a small figure—as formerly, at least, in a conventional bond or preferred stock—he must demand convincing evidence that he is not risking a substantial part of his principal." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 523 (L22472–22481)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Have I written down the calculation — prices, volumes, costs, capital — and does it show a fair chance of a reasonable profit on conservative inputs; and is the downside (what I can lose) small relative to the upside?
- [V] Principle 4: "A fourth business rule is more positive: “Have the courage of your knowledge and experience. If you have formed a conclusion from the facts and if you know your judgment is sound, act on it—even though others may hesitate or differ.” (You are neither right nor wrong because the crowd disagrees with you. You are right because your data and reasoning are right.) Similarly, in the world of securities, courage becomes the supreme virtue after adequate knowledge and a tested judgment are at hand." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 524 (L22484–22491)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Is my conclusion built on data and reasoning I can lay out, so that neither the crowd's enthusiasm for a sector nor its indifference to it is what is deciding for me?
- [V] Closing sentence of the section: "To achieve satisfactory investment results is easier than most people realize; to achieve superior results is harder than it looks." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 524 (L22496–22498)
- [V] Resolved conflict from the first session: the sentence "For the investor this rule should determine the conditions under which he will permit someone else to decide what is done with his money" belongs to the **second** principle, not the first (the search tool had attached it to the first). — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 523

**(c) Margin of safety as the central concept**
- [V] Chapter title: "“Margin of Safety” as the Central Concept of Investment". Opening: "Confronted with a like challenge to distill the secret of sound investment into three words, we venture the motto, MARGIN OF SAFETY. This is the thread that runs through all the preceding discussion of investment policy". — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 512 (L21982–21993)
- [V] For bonds, measured two ways. By earnings: "a railroad should have earned its total fixed charges better than five times (before income tax), taking a period of years, for its bonds to qualify as investment-grade issues." By value: "If the business owes $10 million and is fairly worth $30 million, there is room for a shrinkage of two-thirds in value—at least theoretically—before the bondholders will suffer loss." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, pp. 512–513 (L21996–22041)
- [V] Its function: "Here the function of the margin of safety is, in essence, that of rendering unnecessary an accurate estimate of the future. If the margin is a large one, then it is enough to assume that future earnings will not fall far below those of the past". — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 513 (L22030–22034)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): If sales come in well below plan or costs well above, does the business still cover its fixed obligations — rent, wages, loan payments — or does the plan work only if my forecast is right?
- [V] For ordinary common stocks: "the margin of safety lies in an expected earning power considerably above the going rate for bonds." His illustration, carried over from earlier editions: "Assume in a typical case that the earning power is 9% on the price and that the bond rate is 4%; then the stockbuyer will have an average annual margin of 5% accruing in his favor"; "Over a ten-year period the typical excess of stock earning power over bond interest may aggregate 50% of the price paid." He then says that under 1972 conditions this margin was no longer present. — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, pp. 514–516 (L22073–22135)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Does the expected return on the capital I commit exceed what I could earn safely (for example on Israeli government bonds or a deposit) by enough to pay for the things that will go wrong?
- [V] Paying too much for quality, and where the chief losses come from: "However, the risk of paying too high a price for good-quality stocks—while a real one—is not the chief hazard confronting the average buyer of securities. Observation over many years has taught us that the chief losses to investors come from the purchase of low-quality securities at times of favorable business conditions. The purchasers view the current good earnings as equivalent to “earning power” and assume that prosperity is synonymous with safety." In such times "common stocks of obscure companies can be floated at prices far above the tangible investment, on the strength of two or three years of excellent growth." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 516 (L22163–22175)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Am I judging this industry (or the business I am offered) on two or three boom years, and would the same figures look attractive across a full cycle including a downturn?
- [V] Testing through a bad period: earnings coverage "must be tested over a number of years, including preferably a period of subnormal business such as in 1970–71"; "most of the fair-weather investments, acquired at fair-weather prices, are destined to suffer disturbing price declines when the horizon clouds over". (The phrase "unfavorable business conditions" does not occur in the book; this passage and the one above are where the idea appears.) — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, pp. 516–517 (L22177–22190)
- [V] Growth and price: "The danger in a growth-stock program lies precisely here. For such favored issues the market has a tendency to set prices that will not be adequately protected by a conservative projection of future earnings. (It is a basic rule of prudent investment that all estimates, when they differ from past performance, must err at least slightly on the side of understatement.) The margin of safety is always dependent on the price paid. It will be large at one price, small at some higher price, nonexistent at some still higher price." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 517 (L22205–22213)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): At the price of entry I am facing — purchase price, key money, franchise fee, build-out cost — is there still a cushion if I deliberately understate the growth forecast? At what entry cost does the cushion disappear?
- [V] Bargains: "We have here, by definition, a favorable difference between price on the one hand and indicated or appraised value on the other. That difference is the safety margin. It is available for absorbing the effect of miscalculations or worse than average luck." Even "a moderate decline in the earning power need not prevent the investment from showing satisfactory results." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, pp. 517–518 (L22222–22237)
- [V] Diversification is its companion: "Even with a margin in the investor’s favor, an individual security may work out badly. For the margin guarantees only that he has a better chance for profit than for loss—not that loss is impossible. But as the number of such commitments is increased the more certain does it become that the aggregate of the profits will exceed the aggregate of the losses." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 518 (L22241–22247)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): A founder usually has one venture, not twenty, so Graham's diversification is not available — how much of my net worth is in this single commitment, and can I survive its failure?
- [V] Price can make a mediocre asset sound: "a sufficiently low price can turn a security of mediocre quality into a sound investment opportunity—provided that the buyer is informed and experienced and that he practices adequate diversification." — [Graham][ii], ch. 20, p. 521 (L22356–22359)
- [V] Graham says nothing in chapter 20 specifically about new ventures or start-ups. The nearest statements are the "obscure companies ... floated ... on the strength of two or three years of excellent growth" passage above and his treatment of new issues under (h). — [Graham][ii], ch. 20

**(d) The seven statistical criteria for the defensive investor**
- [V] Purpose: to obtain "(1) a minimum of quality in the past performance and current financial position of the company, and also (2) a minimum of quantity in terms of earnings and assets per dollar of price." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 348 (L15143–15149)
- [V] "1. Adequate Size of the Enterprise": "All our minimum figures must be arbitrary and especially in the matter of size required. Our idea is to exclude small companies which may be subject to more than average vicissitudes especially in the industrial field." Floor: "not less than $100 million of annual sales for an industrial company and, not less than $50 million of total assets for a public utility." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 348 (L15152–15160)
- [V] "2. A Sufficiently Strong Financial Condition": "For industrial companies current assets should be at least twice current liabilities—a so-called two-to-one current ratio. Also, long-term debt should not exceed the net current assets (or “working capital”). For public utilities the debt should not exceed twice the stock equity (at book value)." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 348 (L15162–15167)
- [V] "3. Earnings Stability": "Some earnings for the common stock in each of the past ten years." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 348
- [V] "4. Dividend Record": "Uninterrupted payments for at least the past 20 years." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 348
- [V] "5. Earnings Growth": "A minimum increase of at least one-third in per-share earnings in the past ten years using three-year averages at the beginning and end." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 348
- [V] "6. Moderate Price/Earnings Ratio": "Current price should not be more than 15 times average earnings of the past three years." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 349
- [V] "7. Moderate Ratio of Price to Assets": price not more than 1½ times the book value last reported (the text prints the fraction "1 1⁄2"). "However, a multiplier of earnings below 15 could justify a correspondingly higher multiplier of assets. As a rule of thumb we suggest that the product of the multiplier times the ratio of price to book value should not exceed 22.5. (This figure corresponds to 15 times earnings and 1½ times book value. It would admit an issue selling at only 9 times earnings and 2.5 times asset value, etc.)" — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, p. 349 (L15188–15196)
- [V] His own comment: "They will eliminate the great majority of common stocks"; "Of these tests the most severe under recent financial conditions are those of financial strength." The last two criteria rest on "the absence of an adequate factor of safety when too large a portion of the price must depend on ever-increasing earnings in the future." On the growth test: without it "the typical company would show retrogression, at least in terms of profit per dollar of invested capital." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14, pp. 349–350 (L15198–15222)
- [V] The same seven are previewed at the end of chapter 13 in a slightly different order ("1. Adequate size. 2. A sufficiently strong financial condition. 3. Continued dividends for at least the past 20 years. 4. No earnings deficit in the past ten years. ..."). — [Graham][ii], ch. 13 (L14741–14747)
  - Adaptation (not Graham), for criteria 1–5: Do the established businesses in this Israeli industry (or the one I might buy) show liquid assets of about twice short-term debts, no loss-making year in ten, and real growth in profit over a decade — and if even the incumbents cannot meet these, why would a newcomer?
  - Adaptation (not Graham), for criteria 6–7: If I am buying an existing business, how many years of its three-year-average profit am I paying, and how far above the money actually invested in it (net assets)? Graham's ceilings for a conservative buyer of large listed US companies were 15 and 1.5, product 22.5; his dollar size floors are 1970s US figures and do not transfer.

**(e) The enterprising investor's criteria**
- [V] Starting screen: "a low price in relation to recent earnings" — "stocks that sold at a multiple of nine or less at the end of 1970." Then "some additional criteria, rather similar to those we suggested for the defensive investor, but not so severe". — [Graham][ii], ch. 15, p. 385 (L16599–16622)
- [V] The five additional criteria, verbatim: "1. Financial condition: (a) Current assets at least 1½ times current liabilities, and (b) debt not more than 110% of net current assets (for industrial companies). 2. Earnings stability: No deficit in the last five years covered in the Stock Guide. 3. Dividend record: Some current dividend. 4. Earnings growth: Last year’s earnings more than those of 1966. 5. Price: Less than 120% net tangible assets." (The text prints "1 1⁄2".) — [Graham][ii], ch. 15, pp. 385–386 (L16624–16644)
- [V] On size: "Note also that we set no lower limit on the size of the enterprise. Small companies may afford enough safety if bought carefully and on a group basis." Result: the list of 20 candidates "is reduced to only five". His conclusion: "a group of issues, of at least average quality, meeting criteria of financial condition as well, purchasable at a low multiplier of current earnings and below asset value, should offer good promise of satisfactory investment results." — [Graham][ii], ch. 15, p. 386 (L16648–16679)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): If I enter by acquisition, is the business available at a low multiple of current profit and close to the value of its tangible assets, with no loss in five years and manageable debt — that is, am I buying existing earning power cheaply instead of paying for a story?
- [V] Bargain threshold: "We define a bargain issue as one which, on the basis of facts established by analysis, appears to be worth considerably more than it is selling for"; "an issue is not a true “bargain” unless the indicated value is at least 50% more than the price." — [Graham][ii], ch. 7 (L7715–7721)
- [V] Condition for above-average results: "To enjoy a reasonable chance for continued better than average results, the investor must follow policies which are (1) inherently sound and promising, and (2) not popular on Wall Street." (This sentence is in chapter 1; the first-session notes had placed it in chapter 7.) — [Graham][ii], ch. 1 (L1598–1601)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Is the opportunity sound on its merits and also not the thing everyone in Israel is currently rushing into? If it is fashionable, what do I see that the crowd has not already priced into rents, salaries and acquisition prices?
- [V] Enterprising status demands knowledge on a business footing: "The aggressive investor must have a considerable knowledge of security values—enough, in fact, to warrant viewing his security operations as equivalent to a business enterprise. There is no room in this philosophy for a middle ground, or a series of gradations, between the passive and aggressive status." — [Graham][ii], ch. 7, pp. 175–176 (L8105–8111)
- [V] Defensive versus enterprising, defined: the defensive investor "will place his chief emphasis on the avoidance of serious mistakes or losses. His second aim will be freedom from effort, annoyance, and the need for making frequent decisions. The determining trait of the enterprising (or active, or aggressive) investor is his willingness to devote time and care to the selection of securities that are both sound and more attractive than the average." — [Graham][ii], Introduction, p. 6 (L552–559)
- [V] Return follows effort, not risk: "The rate of return sought should be dependent, rather, on the amount of intelligent effort the investor is willing and able to bring to bear on his task." — [Graham][ii], ch. 4 (L4216–4218)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Am I prepared to put in full-time, informed effort on this business? If I only want a passive stake, I should expect a passive investor's return and should not take an operator's risk.
- [V] Defensive investor's four rules for common stocks: "1. There should be adequate though not excessive diversification. This might mean a minimum of ten different issues and a maximum of about thirty. 2. Each company selected should be large, prominent, and conservatively financed." "3. Each company should have a long record of continuous dividend payments." "4. The investor should impose some limit on the price he will pay for an issue in relation to its average earnings over, say, the past seven years. We suggest that this limit be set at 25 times such average earnings, and not more than 20 times those of the last twelve-month period." — [Graham][ii], ch. 5, pp. 114–115 (L5299–5332)
- [V] "Conservatively financed" defined: "An industrial company’s finances are not conservative unless the common stock (at book value) represents at least half of the total capitalization, including all bank debt. For a railroad or public utility the figure should be at least 30%." — [Graham][ii], ch. 5, p. 122 (L5651–5655)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Will at least half of the capital in the business be equity, with all bank debt counted — or does the plan depend on borrowing more than the owners put in?

**(f) Mr. Market**
- [V] The parable: "Imagine that in some private business you own a small share that cost you $1,000. One of your partners, named Mr. Market, is very obliging indeed. Every day he tells you what he thinks your interest is worth and furthermore offers either to buy you out or to sell you an additional interest on that basis. Sometimes his idea of value appears plausible and justified by business developments and prospects as you know them. Often, on the other hand, Mr. Market lets his enthusiasm or his fears run away with him, and the value he proposes seems to you a little short of silly." — [Graham][ii], ch. 8, pp. 204–205 (L9319–9329)
- [V] The owner's response: "If you are a prudent investor or a sensible businessman, will you let Mr. Market’s daily communication determine your view of the value of a $1,000 interest in the enterprise? Only in case you agree with him, or in case you want to trade with him. You may be happy to sell out to him when he quotes you a ridiculously high price, and equally happy to buy from him when his price is low. But the rest of the time you will be wiser to form your own ideas of the value of your holdings, based on full reports from the company about its operations and financial position." — [Graham][ii], ch. 8, p. 205 (L9331–9338)
- [V] Never a forced seller: "The true investor scarcely ever is forced to sell his shares, and at all other times he is free to disregard the current price quotation." And: "the investor who permits himself to be stampeded or unduly worried by unjustified market declines in his holdings is perversely transforming his basic advantage into a basic disadvantage." — [Graham][ii], ch. 8, p. 203 (L9250–9256)
- [V] What price swings are for: "Basically, price fluctuations have only one significant meaning for the true investor. They provide him with an opportunity to buy wisely when prices fall sharply and to sell wisely when they advance a great deal. At other times he will do better if he forgets about the stock market and pays attention to his dividend returns and to the operating results of his companies." — [Graham][ii], ch. 8, p. 205 (L9348–9354)
- [V] Related practical advice in the same chapter: the conservative investor might "concentrate on issues selling at a reasonably close approximation to their tangible-asset value—say, at not more than one-third above that figure." — [Graham][ii], ch. 8 (L9091–9094)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Is my financing arranged so that I am never forced to sell or close at a bad moment (short-term debt falling due, a partner's exit right, a lease break), and do I value the business from its own operating results instead of from the latest valuation headline or a competitor's funding round?

**(g) Private business, part-ownership and valuing as an owner**
- [V] The shareholder's "double status": "On the one hand his position is analogous to that of a minority shareholder or silent partner in a private business. Here his results are entirely dependent on the profits of the enterprise or on a change in the underlying value of its assets. He would usually determine the value of such a private-business interest by calculating his share of the net worth as shown in the most recent balance sheet." — [Graham][ii], ch. 8, p. 197 (L9012–9021)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): If I am offered a share in an existing private Israeli business, what is my proportionate share of its balance-sheet net worth, and how much above that am I being asked to pay — and for what earning power?
- [V] An unquoted holding is not thereby safer: "it is self-deception to tell yourself that you have suffered no shrinkage in value merely because your securities have no quoted market at all." — [Graham][ii], ch. 8, p. 204 (L9283–9285)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Because a private business has no daily price, am I checking honestly and regularly whether its underlying value has fallen, or assuming it is worth what I paid?
- [V] Control changes the price that is justified: outside investors should buy secondary companies only "for less than their value to a private owner, that is, on a bargain basis", but "Anyone who can control a secondary company, or who is part of a cohesive group with such control, is fully justified in buying the shares on the same basis as if he were investing in a “close corporation” or other private business." And: "In secondary companies the average market value of a detached share is substantially less than its worth to a controlling owner." — [Graham][ii], ch. 7, pp. 177–178 (L8202–8218)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Will I hold control, or a minority stake without it? A minority share in a small private company is worth materially less than its pro-rata slice; have I priced that in and secured rights (information, veto, exit)?
- [V] Owner's view at balance-sheet cost: of two companies selling near asset value Graham says the buyer can "consider himself basically a part owner of these businesses, at a cost corresponding to what the balance sheet shows to be the money invested therein." Similarly, for utilities, "a part owner of well-established and well-earning businesses." — [Graham][ii], ch. 13 (L14734–14737) and ch. 14, p. 359 (L15541–15543)
- [V] Buy versus build — a descriptive remark only, made while explaining takeover premiums: "It often appears good business for such an enterprise to acquire an existing company in the field it wishes to enter rather than to start a new venture from scratch. In order to make such acquisition possible ... it is almost always necessary to offer a price considerably above the current level." — [Graham][ii], ch. 7 (L8060–8066)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Have I compared entering by buying an existing operator (and the premium that requires) with building from scratch (and the time and losses that requires)?

**(h) New issues, "hot" issues, and forecasting industry growth**
- [V] General rule: "Our one recommendation is that all investors should be wary of new issues—which means, simply, that these should be subjected to careful examination and unusually severe tests before they are purchased. There are two reasons for this double caveat. The first is that new issues have special salesmanship behind them, which calls therefore for a special degree of sales resistance. The second is that most new issues are sold under “favorable market conditions”—which means favorable for the seller and consequently less favorable for the buyer." — [Graham][ii], ch. 6, p. 139 (L6409–6422)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Who is selling me this opportunity (broker, franchisor, seller of the business), what do they earn if I say yes, and why is it being offered now — because conditions favour the seller?
- [V] The pattern of new common-stock offerings (Graham quoting his 1959 edition): in bull markets "quite a number of privately owned concerns can be found that are enjoying excellent results—although most of these would not present too impressive a record if the figures were carried back, say, ten years or more." As the rise continues "the quality of the companies becomes steadily poorer; the prices asked and obtained verge on the exorbitant." Outcome: "The heedlessness of the public and the willingness of selling organizations to sell whatever may be profitably sold can have only one result—price collapse. In many cases the new issues lose 75% and more of their offering price." — [Graham][ii], ch. 6, pp. 142–143 (L6548–6576)
- [V] His rule of conduct: "An elementary requirement for the intelligent investor is an ability to resist the blandishments of salesmen offering new common-stock issues during bull markets. Even if one or two can be found that can pass severe tests of quality and value, it is probably bad policy to get mixed up in this sort of business." And: "For every dollar you make in this way you will be lucky if you end up by losing only two." He adds that some "may prove excellent buys—a few years later, when nobody wants them and they can be had at a small fraction of their true worth." — [Graham][ii], ch. 6, p. 143 (L6581–6594)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Is this sector at the stage where everyone is opening the same kind of business on the strength of a few good years? Would it be cheaper and safer to enter two or three years later by buying the survivors' assets at a fraction of today's cost?
- [V] "Hot issues": 1961–62 "saw a complete debacle in a host of newly launched common stocks of small enterprises—the so-called hot issues—which had been offered to the public at ridiculously high prices and then had been further pushed up by needless speculation to levels little short of insane. Many of these lost 90% and more of the quotations in just a few months." — [Graham][ii], ch. 3 (L3640–3645)
- [V] Same point in chapter 15: "When the going is good and new issues are readily salable, stock offerings of no quality at all make their appearance." The enterprising investor "will also be wary of all kinds of new issues, including convertible bonds and preferreds that seem quite tempting and common stocks with excellent earnings confined to the recent past." — [Graham][ii], ch. 15 (L16915–16917) and ch. 6, p. 134 (L6196–6199)
- [V] The airline example. The prevalent view is that successful investing "lies first in the choice of those industries that are most likely to grow in the future and then in identifying the most promising companies in these industries"; "But this is not as easy as it always looks in retrospect." On air transport: "It was, of course, easy to forecast that the volume of air traffic would grow spectacularly over the years." "But despite the expansion of revenues—at a pace even greater than in the computer industry—a combination of technological problems and overexpansion of capacity made for fluctuating and even disastrous profit figures. In the year 1970, despite a new high in traffic figures, the airlines sustained a loss of some $200 million for their shareholders. (They had shown losses also in 1945 and 1961.)" — [Graham][ii], Introduction, pp. 6–7 (L568–612)
- [V] The computer counter-example: funds held no more than "say, 3% of their funds" in IBM because of "its apparently high price and the impossibility of being certain about its rate of growth", and "many—if not most—of their investments in computer-industry companies other than IBM appear to have been unprofitable." — [Graham][ii], Introduction, p. 7 (L614–622)
- [V] The two morals, verbatim: "1. Obvious prospects for physical growth in a business do not translate into obvious profits for investors. 2. The experts do not have dependable ways of selecting and concentrating on the most promising companies in the most promising industries." — [Graham][ii], Introduction, p. 7 (L623–628)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): Demand in this Israeli sector may obviously be growing — but who captures the profit? What do capacity additions by rivals, technology costs and price competition do to margins as volume grows, and are incumbents actually earning money today?
- [V] Zweig's footnote to this passage (Zweig, not Graham): "It is commonly accepted today that the cumulative earnings of the airline industry over its entire history have been negative. The lesson Graham is driving at is not that you should avoid buying airline stocks, but that you should never succumb to the “certainty” that any industry will outperform all others in the future." — [Graham][ii], Introduction, footnote pp. 6–7 (L628–634)
- [V] Forecasting error restated: "The possibility of outright error in the prediction—which we illustrated by our airlines example on p. 6—is no doubt greater than when dealing with near-term earnings." Even a correct forecast may not pay, because "the current market price may already fully reflect what he is anticipating." — [Graham][ii], ch. 1 (L1578–1592)
- [V] Prediction versus protection: analysts convinced that long-term prospects are unusually favourable "will almost always recommend the stock for purchase without paying too much regard to the level at which it is selling. Such, for example, was the general attitude with respect to the air-transport stocks—an attitude that persisted for many years despite the distressingly bad results often shown after 1946." — [Graham][ii], ch. 14 (L15767–15773)
- [V] Growth valuation formula and his own caveat: "Value = Current (Normal) Earnings × (8.5 plus twice the expected annual growth rate)", where "The growth figure should be that expected over the next seven to ten years." (The multiplication sign is lost in the text conversion.) He later writes: "We should have added a caution somewhat as follows: The valuations of expected high-growth stocks are necessarily on the low side, if we were to assume these growth rates will actually be realized"; the valuer in effect has to "introduce a margin of safety into his calculations—somewhat as an engineer does in his specifications for a structure." First factor affecting the capitalisation rate: "1. General Long-Term Prospects. No one really knows anything about what will happen in the distant future, but analysts and investors have strong views on the subject just the same." The five factor headings are General Long-Term Prospects, Management, Financial Strength and Capital Structure, Dividend Record, Current Dividend Rate. — [Graham][ii], ch. 11 (L12828–12830, L12944, L12984–12991, L13053–13061)
  - Adaptation (not Graham): What growth rate does my plan need over the next seven to ten years to justify the entry cost, and does it still work if growth turns out much lower than I project?

### Inferences
- Graham's four business principles are written as rules for running a business and then applied to securities, so they transfer to an owner-operator with the least translation of anything in the book: know the trade, control who runs it, enter only on arithmetic, act on your own analysis.
- His stock-selection numbers (size, current ratio, earnings record, price multiples) were written for buying listed US shares in the early 1970s. For an entrepreneur they are usable in two ways only: as a test when **buying** an existing business, and as a picture of what a financially sound business in the target industry looks like. Applying them to a start-up idea is the researcher's adaptation, not Graham's claim. Graham himself calls the minimum figures "arbitrary".
- Graham's size test points against small companies for the defensive investor, while chapter 15 sets "no lower limit on the size" for the enterprising investor who buys "carefully and on a group basis". A founder with a single small business has neither the defensive investor's size protection nor the enterprising investor's group diversification — which, on Graham's logic, makes the entry price and the financial cushion carry all the weight.
- Three warnings converge on timing: new issues come when conditions favour the seller (ch. 6), the chief losses come from low-quality assets bought "at times of favorable business conditions" (ch. 20), and a few good recent years are routinely mistaken for earning power (ch. 6, ch. 20). For an entrant, the analogue is to distrust an industry's attractiveness most when it is most widely agreed.
- The airline passage gives a concrete mechanism, not just scepticism: growth in volume combined with "technological problems and overexpansion of capacity" destroyed profit. Capacity discipline and capital intensity are therefore the things to check when a growing industry looks attractive.

### Gaps
- Graham gives no framework for founding a business or choosing an industry to enter as an operator. Phrase searches for "new venture", "private business", "close corporation", "part owner", "businessman", "start a business", "own a business" and "small business" returned only the passages listed under (g); nothing else was found, but the book was not read end to end.
- The phrase "unfavorable business conditions" (either spelling) does not appear in the text; the related material is quoted under (c).
- Not extracted: Graham's bond and preferred-stock coverage standards by industry (ch. 11), his tables applying the seven criteria to the DJIA stocks (ch. 14) and Table 15-1, the appendices, and Zweig's chapter commentaries (only footnotes next to the quoted passages were read, and the one used is labelled as Zweig's).
- The full sentence in which the 1949 air-transport paragraph is introduced appears truncated in the text conversion (it begins "Such an investor may for example be a buyer of air-transport stocks"); the quotes used here are from the complete sentences that follow it.
- Page numbers are given only where a running page number was visible next to the passage; elsewhere only the chapter and the local line number are given.

## Key question 3 — Mauboussin & Callahan, "Measuring the Moat": structure and checklist

### Takeaway
The paper frames a moat as two-dimensional — the size of the positive ROIC-minus-WACC spread and how long it lasts — and works from industry analysis down to firm-specific sources of added value (production, consumer and government advantages), ending in a value-creation checklist. ## Key question 4 — S&P DJI: quantitative moat metrics, methodology and results

### Takeaway
S&P identifies moat companies with three indicators — sustained high ROIC, sustained high gross margin, and high market share — converts them to z-scores, averages them into an "Economic Moat Score", and takes the top 50 S&P 500 companies, equally weighted. It is a **relative ranking**; no absolute threshold (such as "gross margin above X%") surfaced in any excerpt.

### Cited Findings
- [S] The paper's lead contributor is Rupert Watts, CFA, CAIA, Head of Factors and Dividends. It describes the S&P 500 Economic Moat Index, launched April 2024, which tracks S&P 500 companies identified as having a wide economic moat using a purely quantitative approach. — [S&P DJI paper](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/research/research-a-systematic-approach-for-identifying-companies-with-economic-moats.pdf)
- [S] The paper argues that determining a moat requires a comprehensive approach that goes beyond a single quantitative measure, and that metrics should be looked at over multiple periods. — [S&P DJI paper](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/research/research-a-systematic-approach-for-identifying-companies-with-economic-moats.pdf)
- [S] Three core metrics: high ROIC, high gross margins, high market share. On consistency: many companies can generate high returns in the short term, but doing so consistently indicates a wide moat, so the metrics are analysed over multiple periods. — [Indexology, 15 May 2024](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2024/05/15/identifying-economic-moats-using-a-quantitative-lens-the-sp-500-economic-moat-index/)
- [S] Gross margin: two measures are used — gross margin over the past 12 months and gross margin stability over the last 5 years. Purpose: to assess pricing power, i.e. companies able to charge a premium over the cost of production. — [Indexology, 5 June 2024](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2024/06/05/the-quantitative-metrics-for-identifying-an-economic-moat/)
- [S] Market share: a high share may indicate a strong moat from economies of scale, network effects and brand power, but the metric is most useful combined with others, since high share may not indicate high profitability or sustainable advantage. — [Indexology, 5 June 2024](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2024/06/05/the-quantitative-metrics-for-identifying-an-economic-moat/)
- [S] Index rules: starting universe is the S&P 500 excluding the GICS real estate sector; each security receives an Economic Moat score based on gross margin and the 5-year standard deviation of gross margin, the 5-year average of ROIC, and a market share score; the top 50 by score are selected and equally weighted; rebalanced and reconstituted semi-annually. — [index factsheet copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/907194272/Fs-Sp-500-Economic-Moat-Index-Usd)
- [S] The Economic Moat Score is the average of the three indicator z-scores. — [S&P DJI paper](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/research/research-a-systematic-approach-for-identifying-companies-with-economic-moats.pdf)
- [S] The Market Share Score is sourced from Syntax, a financial data company. For GICS financials, gross margin and its 5-year standard deviation are not calculated and are left out of the score. — [First Trust EMOT page](https://www.ftportfolios.com/Retail/etf/etfSummary.aspx?Ticker=EMOT)
- [S] Formulas, from a secondary summary only: ROIC = net income divided by the average of (total equity + total debt) across two periods, averaged over five years; gross margin = (revenue − cost of goods sold) / revenue. — [Finomenon summary](https://finomenon.us/economic-moat-investing-framework/)
- [S] Quintile tests (rebalanced semi-annually in June and December, like the index): long-term annualised returns rose steadily by quintile, with quintile 5 at 14.38% against 10.56% for quintile 1; for sustained gross margin, volatility fell as margin rose and risk-adjusted return improved from 0.48 (quintile 1) to 0.91 (quintile 5). The excerpt did not state which metric the 14.38%/10.56% pair belongs to. — [Indexology, 5 June 2024](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2024/06/05/the-quantitative-metrics-for-identifying-an-economic-moat/)
- [S] Back-test: over the full period starting June 30, 2013, the index outperformed the S&P 500 by 2.63% annualised; the back-test applies the methodology in effect at launch. — [Indexology, 15 May 2024](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2024/05/15/identifying-economic-moats-using-a-quantitative-lens-the-sp-500-economic-moat-index/)
- [S] A secondary source gives risk-adjusted return of 1.10 for the index against 0.91 for the S&P 500; not verified against S&P material. — [Finomenon summary](https://finomenon.us/economic-moat-investing-framework/)
- [S] Sector make-up: Consumer Staples and Consumer Discretionary have been the largest weights (from the Spanish-language version). — [S&P DJI paper, ES](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/es/documents/research/research-a-systematic-approach-for-identifying-companies-with-economic-moats-es.pdf)
- [S] Later live figures from S&P's July 2026 factor dashboard: total return of 16.2% over 3 years, 13.0% over 5 years and 16.2% over 10 years (snapshot only; the excerpt did not say whether the figures are annualised). — [S&P factor dashboard](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/performance-reports/dashboard-sp-500-factor.pdf)

### Inferences
- For an entrepreneur the transferable part is the three tests and the insistence on persistence, not the index: does the business (or its best incumbents) show high gross margin now, has that margin been stable for five years, is return on invested capital high on a five-year average, and is share of the relevant market high alongside profitability.
- Since S&P ranks by z-score within the S&P 500, any numeric cut-off applied to Israeli small businesses would be the analyst's own choice and should be presented as such.
- S&P itself warns that market share alone is weak evidence; it should count only when margin and ROIC also hold.
- Back-tested index results are evidence that these traits coincided with better stock returns in U.S. large caps from 2013; they are not evidence about entry success for a new business.
