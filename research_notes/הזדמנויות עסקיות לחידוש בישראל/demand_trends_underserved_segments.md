## Key question 1 — Which demand pools are largest in NIS and fastest-growing through 2030-2035?

### Takeaway
The biggest documented, structurally growing pools are (a) ageing/long-term care (NIS 23.6bn national LTC spend in 2022, +63% in four years; 65+ population 1.29m heading to ~2m), (b) the renter economy (29.6% of households, average rent ~NIS 5,050/month rising ~3.4% a year against 1.1% CPI), (c) early-childhood care (540,000 children aged 0-3, half outside licensed care), and (d) the Haredi and Arab populations (14.3% and ~21% of the population; Haredim projected to reach 24.4% by 2050). War-driven pools (defence at ~NIS 143bn in 2026, reconstruction NIS 5-15bn, mental health with ~900,000 expected treatment seekers) are large but more policy-dependent.

### Inferences
- [I] Rough annual pool sizes (my arithmetic, not sourced totals): renters ≈ 29.6% × ~3.1m households ≈ 0.9m households × ~NIS 5,050 × 12 ≈ **NIS 55bn/yr of rent**; LTC ≈ NIS 23.6bn in 2022 and, if growth merely halved from the 2018-22 pace, **≈ NIS 30bn by 2026**; ages 0-3 care ≈ 540,000 children × ~55-60% in paid care × NIS 3,000-5,000 × 12 ≈ **NIS 11-19bn/yr**; defence ≈ NIS 143bn (2026) but only a fraction is addressable by a new civilian business.
- [I] Fastest structural growth to 2035: the 75+ cohort (20-30k net additions per year) and the Haredi population (share rising from 14.3% toward ~24% by 2050); both are demographic certainties rather than policy bets. Renting is growing more slowly but steadily (share up every year since 2013) and rent inflation (~3.4%) is running about three times CPI (1.1% in Oct 2026).
- [I] The two "65+ projections" are not fully consistent (≈2m by 2050 per the Comptroller vs +46% by 2040, i.e. ≈1.9m, per Calcalist). Use "≈1.9-2.0m by 2040-2050" and verify against the current CBS projection tables.
- [I] No credible current NIS figure exists for Haredi or Arab consumer spending in the sources found; the published "purchasing power" numbers (NIS 3-10bn) are 8-15 years old and clearly understate a 1.45m-person community. A bottom-up estimate from the CBS Household Expenditure Survey 2023 (released 1 Jan 2026) is needed.

## Key question 2 — Where is the gap between demand and quality of supply best documented?

### Takeaway
The best-documented gaps (official audits, surveys, waiting-time data) are in mental health (waits of 6.5 months to 1.5 years), early childhood care (half of toddlers outside licensed care, ~3,000 missing caregivers), elder care (caregiver shortage, 1.4 sheltered-housing units per 100 seniors, no national ageing plan), specialist medicine (1.2 million people buying private care to skip queues), long-term rental (only ~931 institutional units marketed in 2024) and services to Arab towns (delivery, addresses, credit, mortgages). EV charging, public transport and labour supply in construction are documented but are infrastructure/regulatory bottlenecks rather than easy entry points.

## Key question 4 — Specifically Israeli constraints an entrant must respect, and implications for scale and export

### Takeaway
The market is ~10.2 million people growing under 1% a year with net emigration, split into sub-markets (Haredi 14%, Arab ~21%) that need different channels, payment methods and content rules, and it is exposed to war shocks, reserve duty, labour-quota politics and abrupt regulatory reversals. Scale inside Israel is therefore capped, and models that depend on a single regulated tariff or tax threshold are fragile.

### Cited Findings
- [V] Population 10.178m; 2025 net international migration ≈ −20,000 (69,300 left, 19,000 returned, ~24,600 immigrants); Taub's method gives ≈ −37,000 — [Ynet](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/r1zatkmnzg); [Taub Center](https://www.taubcenter.org.il/en/pr/snr-2025-demography/)
- [E] TAU study: ~270,000 Israelis left for at least three consecutive months in 2023-2025; ~125,000 emigrated in 2022-2024 — [Tel Aviv University](https://english.tau.ac.il/research/emigration-from-israel); [Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/hidden-cost-of-war-125000-israelis-emigrated-between-2022-and-2024/)
- [E] Partial offset: aliyah from North America (~2,700 by late August 2026, >4,000 expected) and France (~2,400 Jan-Jul 2026, +30%) is rising; >20,000 immigrants in Jewish year 5786 — [Algemeiner](https://algemeiner.com/2026/08/27/north-american-aliyah-reaches-record-high-august-thousands-choose-israel-despite-regional-uncertainty); [Ynet](https://www.ynetnews.com/jewish-world/article/b1ltujk8gg)

- [E] State budget priorities crowd out civilian programmes: Arab five-year-plan money diverted to security; northern rehabilitation funds cut/folded into broader lines in the 2026 budget — [Ynet](https://www.ynetnews.com/article/6fc5qls2e); [Times of Israel](https://www.timesofisrael.com/northern-communities-protest-cuts-to-rehabilitation-budget-amid-war/)

### Inferences
- [I] Achievable scale: with ~3.1m households, even 10% national penetration is ~310,000 households; a sub-segment business (e.g., Haredi families ≈ 14% of population, or Arab towns ≈ 21%) is addressing a few hundred thousand households at most. Care businesses scale by labour, and labour is the quota-bound input — capacity, not demand, sets the ceiling.
- [I] Kashrut and Shabbat (general background, not sourced in this session): food and hospitality concepts need kosher certification to reach the Haredi/traditional majority, and Friday-evening-to-Saturday-night closure removes ~1/7 of trading time for a Shabbat-observant brand; operating on Shabbat excludes Haredi customers. These should be verified by the report writer if used.
- [I] Three language markets (Hebrew, Arabic, Russian) plus Haredi-specific media mean customer acquisition cost does not fall linearly with scale; products need at least Hebrew + Arabic service to reach >95% of the population.
- [I] Export implications: models built on Israel-specific frictions (no-address logistics, foreign-caregiver management, Haredi filtered channels) export only to analogous niches (Haredi communities in NY/London; emerging markets with informal addressing). Models built on universal ageing/childcare/mental-health shortages can export if the core asset is software or protocol rather than Israeli labour and licences. Businesses whose margin is a regulatory arbitrage (VAT threshold, electricity tender) do not export and can vanish locally with one Knesset vote.
- [I] Net emigration skews toward young, educated, higher-income households (per the framing of the TAU/Globes coverage — not verified in detail), which trims the premium end of domestic consumer demand, while immigration from France/North America adds an affluent, service-hungry, non-Hebrew-speaking niche.

- No sourced data on the Russian-speaking segment size or behaviour in 2025-26.
- Licensing specifics (daycare supervision law, home-care agency licensing, sheltered-housing law, non-bank credit licence) were not researched.

## Key question 5 — Macro context, late 2026

### Takeaway
Macro conditions are benign on paper — policy rate 3.25% after three cuts in 2026, CPI inflation 1.1% (five-year low), a historically strong shekel (~NIS 3.0-3.2 per USD), headline GDP growth forecast of 5.6% for 2026 — but the domestic economy is growing nearer 3.4%, small-business closures run at ~60,000 a year, and record tech funding (USD 7.6bn in H1 2026) is concentrated in late-stage cyber/AI, not local-market consumer businesses.

### Inferences
- [I] Financing implication: a local-market, non-export business should not plan on Israeli VC (which funds cyber/AI at late stage); realistic sources are bank/non-bank credit at prime-plus (prime 4.75%), state-guaranteed loans, strategic investors (HAAT's lead was Israel Post), REIT/real-estate capital for care and rental assets, and angels. Falling rates and low inflation make leveraged, asset-backed models (sheltered housing, rental) more financeable than a year ago.
- [I] A strong shekel (~3.0-3.2) lowers the cost of imported goods and equipment and sustains cross-border shopping pressure on local retail; it favours import-based and service businesses over local manufacturing.
- [I] Headline GDP of 5.6% overstates domestic demand; the ~3.4% domestic figure, falling participation and ~61,000 closures a year indicate a two-speed economy — consumers are price-sensitive (trading down, AliExpress/Temu) even while macro prints look strong.
